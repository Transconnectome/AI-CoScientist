Socioeconomic deprivation is linked to psychiatric risk in children, but who is most affected and why is unclear. In a longitudinal cohort of 2,135 children, we used an instrumental‑variable random forest that exploits state source‑of‑income anti‑discrimination laws to isolate variation in neighborhood adversity. Higher adversity predicted (i) steeper delay discounting—preference for smaller‑sooner over larger‑later rewards—and (ii) more psychotic‑like experiences (subclinical hallucination‑ or paranoia‑like symptoms). Effects varied widely across children, consistent with a behavioral poverty trap, where stress‑biased choices sustain hardship. To explain this heterogeneity, we fit an integrated model combining decision‑making, brain structure and function (prefrontal–limbic morphometry and reward‑task activation), and genetics (polygenic scores for cognitive performance, IQ, and educational attainment). The children most sensitive to neighborhood adversity showed a paradoxical profile: higher genetic propensity for cognitive achievement plus limbic alterations (smaller volumes, heightened reward reactivity) and steeper discounting. This pattern challenges diathesis–stress accounts and supports differential susceptibility. By identifying who is most vulnerable and why, our results inform targeted prevention to reduce childhood psychopathology and break cycles of socioeconomic disadvantage.
