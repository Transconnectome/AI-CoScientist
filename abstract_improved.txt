Socioeconomic deprivation increases psychiatric vulnerability in children, yet why some children are more affected than others remains unclear. We applied an instrumental-variable random-forest framework to longitudinal data from 2,135 children (ages 9-10 at baseline) to estimate how neighborhood socioeconomic deprivation affects impulsive decision-making (delay discounting) and psychotic-like experiences (PLEs), using state source-of-income anti-discrimination laws as natural experiments. Under standard causal assumptions, higher neighborhood adversity increased both delay discounting and PLEs on average, with substantial variation across children—consistent with behavioral poverty traps where poverty-induced distress drives decisions that perpetuate hardship. An integrated model combining delay discounting, prefrontal-limbic brain structure and function, and polygenic scores revealed marked heterogeneity in vulnerability to neighborhood adversity. Strikingly, children most vulnerable to neighborhood effects showed a paradoxical profile: higher cognitive polygenic scores combined with specific limbic alterations (reduced volumes, heightened reward-related activation). This unexpected pattern—where genetic advantages for cognition paradoxically confer vulnerability to environmental adversity—challenges traditional diathesis-stress models and supports differential susceptibility theory. By identifying which children are most vulnerable to neighborhood disadvantage and the biological mechanisms underlying this vulnerability, our findings enable precision approaches to preventing childhood psychopathology and disrupting cycles of socioeconomic disadvantage.
