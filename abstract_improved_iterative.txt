Neighborhood poverty increases psychiatric risk in children, but which children are most vulnerable remains unknown. We examined 2,135 children longitudinally using a novel causal inference approach that exploits state anti-discrimination housing laws as natural experiments. Higher neighborhood deprivation increased impulsive decision-making and psychotic-like experiences (hallucination-like perceptions and paranoid thoughts). However, vulnerability varied dramatically across children based on an integrated model combining brain structure, reward-related brain function, and genetic factors. 

Paradoxically, children most susceptible to neighborhood adversity showed higher genetic propensity for cognitive achievement combined with specific brain alterations: reduced limbic volumes yet heightened reward-related activation. This pattern challenges traditional models assuming genetic risk amplifies environmental harm, instead supporting differential susceptibility theory—wherein the same children most responsive to negative environments may also benefit most from positive ones.

By identifying which children are vulnerable to neighborhood disadvantage and the underlying neurocognitive mechanisms, these findings enable targeted early interventions to prevent psychiatric disorders and disrupt intergenerational cycles of poverty. Our precision-medicine framework offers a pathway from identifying at-risk children to actionable prevention strategies.
