Children with genetic advantages for cognition are paradoxically *more* vulnerable to neighborhood poverty's effects on mental health—a finding that challenges conventional wisdom about who is at risk. We investigated why socioeconomic deprivation affects some children more than others by analyzing longitudinal data from 2,135 children (ages 9-10 at baseline), using state anti-discrimination policy changes as natural experiments. Higher local deprivation increased psychotic-like experiences, with threefold variation in susceptibility across children. An integrated model combining brain imaging, genetics, and decision-making revealed that the most susceptible children showed an unexpected profile: elevated cognitive polygenic scores paired with specific limbic alterations (reduced volumes, heightened reward activation). This pattern supports differential susceptibility theory, where the same neural characteristics enabling cognitive advantages under favorable conditions become liabilities under adversity. Deprivation also increased impulsive decision-making—consistent with stress-driven choices that perpetuate hardship. By identifying which children are most affected by socioeconomic disadvantage and the biological pathways involved, these findings enable targeted interventions that match support intensity to individual susceptibility, potentially disrupting intergenerational cycles of poverty and psychopathology.
