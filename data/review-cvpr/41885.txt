GeoMAMBA-AD: Early Detection of Alzheimer’s Disease Using Non-Invasive
Resting-State fMRI and John Ellipsoid-Driven MAMBA Network
Anonymous CVPR submission
Abstract
Early detection of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is crucial for 001
timely clinical treatment. Resting-state functional magnetic 002
resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) can reveal early functional 003
connectivity alterations that precede structural changes, 004
but its use is constrained by limited data availability and 005
scanner-induced domain shift. To address these challenges, 006
we propose GeoMAMBA-AD, a framework that integrates 007
John ellipsoid-based (JE) blind source separation (BSS) 008
with a Mamba-based temporal learner to achieve robust 009
cross-scanner generalization under small-data conditions. 010
Exploiting a convex geometry formulation, JE can prov- 011
ably extract the perfect sources under rather mild data pu- 012
rity conditions, while avoiding the NP-hard computing and 013
unreliable source independence assumptions used in ICA. 014
Moreover, JE-BSS is performed at the individual-subject 015
level, enabling source separation without enforcing shared 016
component structures across subjects (e.g., group ICA). 017
The resulting geometric embeddings are subsequently pro- 018
cessed by a Mamba network, enabling stable and accurate 019
AD classification across heterogeneous rs-fMRI datasets. 020
GeoMAMBA-AD achieves 92% accuracy in cross-scanner 021
scenarios under small-data learning settings, underscor- 022
ing its robustness across different fMRI scanners and ac- 023
quisition protocols. Furthermore, gradient-based inter- 024
pretability analysis reveals that the geometry sources cap- 025
ture known executive control and subcortical control sys- 026
tems, both highly correlated to AD biomarkers, supporting 027
the neurobiological plausibility of the JE sources. This is 028
the first work that integrates geometry BSS into a Mamba 029
backbone for early AD detection.030
1. Introduction031
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenera- 032
tive disease and the most common cause of dementia. Ac- 033
cording to the World Health Organization (WHO), more 034
than 55 million people currently live with dementia, and this 035number is expected to nearly triple by 2050 due to global036
population aging [1]. AD is characterized by the gradual037
decline of cognitive functions, including memory, reason-038
ing, and language, ultimately leading to severe functional039
impairment and loss of independence. Given the absence040
of curative treatments, early and accurate detection of AD041
is crucial. Detecting AD in its prodromal stages enables042
timely clinical interventions that can slow disease progres-043
sion and improve patients’ quality of life, thereby reducing044
the socioeconomic burden on caregivers and healthcare sys-045
tems. Recent therapeutic advances, such as the monoclonal046
antibody donanemab approved by the United States Food047
and Drug Administration in 2024 for early symptomatic048
AD, which targets amyloid pathology to slow clinical pro-049
gression, further underscore the critical need for early di-050
agnosis to maximize treatment efficacy [2]. Advanced AD051
research has associated the disease with several brain ab-052
normalities, including amyloid-β(Aβ) plaques, hyperphos-053
phorylatedτ(p-tau) tangles, and myelin breakdown [3].054
These changes often lead to the shrinkage of brain regions,055
notably the hippocampus, and altered neuronal communi-056
cation within cortical networks.057
Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging058
(rs-fMRI) is a prevalent neuroimaging technique used to de-059
tect these changes. It measures spontaneous blood-oxygen-060
level-dependent (BOLD) signal fluctuations throughout061
the brain without requiring task performance [4]. Stud-062
ies have demonstrated positive correlations between AD-063
related cognitive decline and changes in BOLD signals064
measured by rs-fMRI [5–8]. Specifically, brain regions065
affected by neurodegeneration exhibit distinct BOLD sig-066
nal patterns in AD patients compared to healthy individu-067
als. This pathology-related difference makes rs-fMRI suit-068
able for early AD detection. However, unlike structural069
MRI (sMRI), which has been extensively investigated for070
Alzheimer’s disease diagnosis [9–13], rs-fMRI introduces071
substantially greater algorithmic and methodological chal-072
lenges due to its dynamic, temporally fluctuating neural sig-073
nals. Specifically, its BOLD signals are indirect reflections074
of neural activity and are easily confounded by head motion,075

dermining model generalization. To address this chal- 097
lenge, we introduce a John ellipsoid–based blind source 098
separation [24]. In convex geometry, the John ellip- 099
soid defines an affine-invariant mapping that normalizes 100
each domain’s feature distribution. Such normalization, 101
when combined with individual-subject BSS, mitigates 102
scanner-dependent variability and improves cross-scanner 103
consistency in rs-fMRI analysis. This mapping minimizes 104
domain-specific anisotropy and aligns heterogeneous scan- 105
ners’ feature spaces, yielding a unified representation well- 106
suited for downstream modeling. Meanwhile, the blind 107
source separation step produces corresponding temporal 108
dynamic curves (TDCs) that are subsequently processed 109
through fully connected layers to encode the temporal infor- 110
mation of rs-fMRI signals, while the spatial sources are fed 111
into the Mamba network for feature extraction. This dual- 112
path design enables the framework to capture spatial and 113
temporal dynamics independently, obtaining the comple- 114
mentary information more effectively under multi-scanner 115
and small-data conditions. In the next section, we introduce 116
several seminal methods that have been developed for pro- 117
cessing rs-fMRI data.118
2. Related Work119
2.1. Resting-State fMRI Analysis for AD120
Traditional approaches typically rely on independent com- 121
ponent analysis (ICA) [25] or related correlation-based 122
measures to characterize brain connectivity alterations [26]. 123
More recently, deep learning–based models such as 3D 124
convolutional neural networks (3D-CNNs), recurrent neu- 125
ral networks (RNNs), and long short-term memory network 126have been explored for automated AD classification using127
rs-fMRI data [9, 10, 27]. In particular, the brain connec-128
tivity graph convolutional network adopts a graph convolu-129
tional network (GCN) to encode inter-regional brain con-130
nectivity based on rs-fMRI signals [28]. Specifically, the131
method employs the anatomical automatic labeling (AAL)132
atlas to define 90 regions of interest (ROIs) and constructs133
subject-specific adjacency matrices by computing pairwise134
functional connectivity (i.e., Pearson correlation) between135
ROI time series. This design allows the GCN to exploit136
both node-level features and topological priors encoded in137
the graph structure, providing a relational inductive bias that138
can enhance generalization in limited-data settings. Spa-139
tiotemporal graph transformer extends this design by simul-140
taneously incorporating both node-level activation features141
and inter-regional connectivity [29]. Specifically, it utilizes142
the anatomical automatic labeling (AAL) template to ex-143
tract 116 regions of interest (ROIs) and constructs subject-144
wise relation matrices based on ROI correlations. These145
relation matrices are subsequently used to initialize the at-146
tention weights within a spatiotemporal transformer block,147
allowing the model to encode prior structural dependen-148
cies directly into the attention mechanism and jointly learn149
spatial–temporal representations from rs-fMRI data. An-150
other representative approach, gICA 3D-VGG [30], com-151
bines group-wise ICA (gICA) with a 3D-CNN to extract152
shared functional components across subjects and perform153
volumetric classification. However, these models could re-154
quire large-scale datasets and tend to exhibit limited gener-155
alization across scanners due to signal variability, scanner156
drift, and demographic heterogeneity.157
2.1.1. Blind Source Separation and Convex Geometry in158
Neuroimaging159
Blind source separation (BSS) has its roots in hyperspec-160
tral imaging and remote sensing, where geometry-based161
formulations have been developed to decompose mixed162
spectral signals into constituent material signatures with-163
out prior distributional assumptions [24, 31–34]. Unlike164
ICA, geometry-based BSS methods emphasize the con-165
vex and spatial separability of sources and therefore do166
not rely on statistical independence assumptions. When167
applied to rs-fMRI, this property allows geometry-based168
BSS to separate spatially overlapping but functionally re-169
lated brain sources that would otherwise be confounded170
by ICA. Recent convex-geometry formulations further en-171
hance BSS by introducing the John ellipsoid (JE), defined172
as the maximum-volume ellipsoid inscribed within a data173
convex hull, serving as an affine-invariant preconditioning174
scheme. In this work, JE-BSS is performed at the individual175
subject level, enabling source extraction without requiring176
cross-subject alignment or group-level factorization, which177
is particularly important when dealing with heterogeneous178
clinical cohorts.179

2.2. Mamba and Selective State-Space Model180
Learning the spatial representations of functional brain 181
sources is essential for identifying disease-related alter- 182
ations in rs-fMRI data. While transformer-based architec- 183
tures have shown strong representational power for captur- 184
ing spatial dependencies, their quadratic complexity limits 185
scalability for small and high-dimensional fMRI datasets 186
[29]. The recently proposed Mamba architecture, based 187
on a selective state-space model (SSM), enables efficient 188
long-range dependency learning with linear computational 189
complexity [17]. Building upon these insights, we extend 190
Mamba to functional imaging by coupling it with a JE-BSS 191
framework, described in detail in Section 3.192
3. Method193
3.1. Method Overview194
Conventional deep learning models trained on rs-fMRI of- 195
ten overfit to scanner-specific acquisition statistics under 196
limited sample sizes, leading to shortcut learning that ex- 197
ploits scanner-dependent cues rather than disease-relevant 198
neural patterns. To mitigate this limitation, we propose 199
GeoMAMBA-AD, a hybrid framework that integrates a 200
non-trainable John ellipsoid–based blind source separation 201
(JE-BSS) stage with a Mamba-based spatiotemporal learn- 202
ing network. The JE-BSS module serves as a geometry- 203
driven convex preconditioning stage, enforcing scanner- 204
independent source separation through John ellipsoid pre- 205
conditioning and simplex-constrained optimization. The 206
spatial sources are then fed into the Mamba branch for fea- 207
ture learning, while their associated TDCs, estimated via 208
convex optimization (CVX), are processed by fully con- 209
nected layers to encode temporal dependencies.210
An overview of the proposed GeoMAMBA-AD pipeline 211
is shown in Fig. 1. We describe each step in detail as fol- 212
lows, covering both the non-trainable BSS stage and the 213
trainable Mamba stage.214

4.3. Quantitative Results403
We evaluate GeoMAMBA-AD under a realistic clinical de- 404
ployment scenario characterized by scanner-induced do- 405
main shift. The definitions of all evaluation metrics are 406
provided in the supplementary material. This experiment 407
examines whether the model can learn disease-relevant tem- 408
poral representations rather than relying on scanner-specific 409
statistics, thereby providing a more faithful measure of clin- 410
ical generalizability. This setup reflects a common chal- 411
lenge in neuroimaging research, where increasing sample 412
size across clinical centers inevitably introduces domain 413
shifts due to scanner-specific hardware and acquisition dis- 414
crepancies. To simulate this, we allocate 75 subjects for 415
training validation and designate 25 subjects (all randomly 416
sampled from each scanner) as a cross-scanner dataset. In 417
practice, this mixed-domain training often leads to the op- 418
posite effect: instead of learning stable disease signatures, 419
the network rapidly locks onto scanner-specific low-level 420
statistics as the most discriminative cues during optimiza- 421
tion. Since scanner-induced intensity distributions are often 422
more dominant and easier to separate than subtle patholog- 423
ical patterns, the model prioritizes domain separation over 424
disease separation, resulting in a latent space that is well- 425
clustered by scanner, but not really aligned with clinical 426
labels. With John ellipsoid–based source separation, the 427
geometry-stabilized latent sources become less sensitive to 428
cross-scanner domain shift, enabling GeoMAMBA-AD to 429
achieve SOTA performance across scanners.430
Compared to this, several peer methods reveal varying 431
degrees of sensitivity to domain shifts. BCGCN, which em- 432
ploys graph convolutional networks to model brain data us- 433
ing only ROI-based features, underperforms in this setting. 434
Its lack of temporal information matrices limits its abil- 435
ity to capture individualized dynamic changes of network 436
structures, thereby reducing generalizability across hetero- 437
geneous domains. STGTN incorporates a spatiotemporal 438
transformer architecture with adjacency initialization (via 439
AAL’s 116-ROI template), graph modeling, and adversarial 440
training. Among all peer methods, it demonstrates the most 441
stable performance under cross-scanner conditions. This 442
suggests that while its architectural components help mit- 443
igate scanner-induced variability, substantial domain shifts 444
still pose challenges even for robust models. Finally, we 445
replicate the gICA 3D-VGG model, which contains over 446
86 million parameters, to evaluate the generalizability of 447group ICA-based approaches. While group ICA is theo-448
retically designed to extract common functional networks449
across subjects by jointly decomposing the entire dataset, its450
effectiveness can be compromised under cross-scanner sce-451
narios. Scanner heterogeneity in signal characteristics may452
distort the group-level decomposition, resulting in compo-453
nents that fail to capture scanner-independent neural pat-454
terns. In our experiments, the gICA 3D-VGG model per-455
forms suboptimally on the cross-scanner dataset, suggesting456
that the representations learned through ICA do not gener-457
alize well across imaging domains—despite their intended458
inter-subject consistency. In contrast, our method (based on459
JE-based BSS and a dual-track structure) achieves higher460
accuracy with only 1976,260 parameters, nearly 4 times461
fewer than the reproduced VGG model. These results un-462
derscore the benefit of combining a source separation ap-463
proach that relaxes the independence assumption with dual-464
track feature learning, enabling robust performance under465
scanner-induced heterogeneity.466
4.4. Ablation of Non-trainable Stage467
In this experiment, we evaluate the robustness of differ-468
ent blind source decomposition strategies used in the non-469
trainable stage of the framework. Specifically, we com-470
pare the widely-used group-wise Independent Component471
Analysis (gICA) with our proposed geometric ellipsoid-472
based blind source separation (JE-BSS). To ensure fair473
comparison, the entire downstream trainable pipeline of474
GeoMAMBA-AD is kept identical across both settings;475
only the non-trainable decomposition module is switched.476
As shown in Table 2, replacing JE-BSS with gICA leads477
to a substantial performance drop (OA: 92.00% down to478
76.00%,κ: 83.87 down to 48.28), confirming that conven-479
tional ICA-based latent source extraction is less stable in480
heterogeneous rs-fMRI scenarios. When comparing against481
peer methods that also rely on gICA-based preprocessing482
(cf. Table 1), we observe that even with the same latent483
source initialization, using a lightweight Mamba backbone484
together with our temporal interaction modeling (cf. Sec-485
tion 4.5 for temporal branch analysis) still yields perfor-486
mance gains (higher precision). This suggests that Mamba487
is naturally suitable for scenarios with limited training sam-488
ples. Although most prior Mamba studies have been con-489
ducted outside the neuroimaging domain, recent work has490
already shown that Mamba-based models can outperform491
CNN architectures under data-scarce medical settings [39].492
Motivated by this, we investigate whether such small-data493
advantages of Mamba can also transfer to rs-fMRI based494
Alzheimer’s disease classification. Our results show that495
this advantage carries over to rs-fMRI, even when the up-496
stream latent decomposition relies on gICA.497

4.6. Gradient-Based Identification of Neural Con- 526
trol Subsystems527
Unlike group-wise statistical decomposition methods that 528
directly project shared group-level spatial patterns, our 529
individual geometry BSS extracts subject-specific latent 530
sources. Therefore, it is necessary to examine whether 531
these individualized sources still reflect known neurobio- 532
logical networks. In this study, we specifically used peak 533
integrated gradients (IG) attribution values [41] to quan- 534
tify which anatomical ROIs contribute most strongly, en- 535
suring that only the most discriminative nodes were consid- 536
ered (detailed implementation is provided in supplementary 537
material). To this end, we applied IG attribution analysis 538
to identify which anatomical ROIs the AI model relied on 539
when making the AD/NC decision based on the geometry- 540
derived source representations. This enables direct inspec- 541
tion of where the model assigns biological evidence during 542
inference, as demonstrated in Fig. 2, where only the first 543
source of each subject is visualized.544
IG attribution maps from the first source of each subject 545
consistently revealed three functional control subsystems, 546each exerting distinct influence on the model’s AD classifi-547
cation decision:548
•Frontoparietal Control Network (FPN) Core:High IG549
contributions were observed in the middle frontal gyrus550
(MFG), superior frontal gyrus (SFG/SFGmed), and infe-551
rior parietal lobule (IPL), as Fig. 2 shown. These cortical552
regions form the canonical FPN core supporting executive553
control. Their strong contributions suggest that disruption554
of top-down control processes provides salient discrimi-555
native cues for AD [42, 43].556
•Fronto–Cingulo–Striatal (FCS) Control Circuit:A557
second subsystem involved the anterior cingulate cor-558
tex (ACC), caudate nucleus (CAU), and superior frontal559
gyrus (SFG), as illustrated in Fig. 2e. This FCS circuit560
corresponds to a cortico–cingulo–striatal executive con-561
trol pathway, which has been linked to frontal–striatal562
contributions to cognitive control and behavioral regula-563
tion [44, 45].564
•Fronto-Striatal-Thalamo (FST) Recurrent Circuit:A565
third cluster included the ACC, CAU, and thalamus566
(THA). This FST circuit corresponds to the canonical567
cortico-basal-ganglia-thalamic recurrent loop (cf. Fig. 2).568
The contribution of this recurrent control loop suggests569
that disruption of closed-loop executive regulation further570
supports AD vs. NC differentiation [46, 47].571
Overall, these results demonstrate that the proposed572
geometry-based source extraction does not produce arbi-573
trary spatial mixtures, but identifies three well-characterized574
executive control subsystems (FPN core, FCS, and FST)575
known to be impacted during early-stage Alzheimer’s dis-576
ease. In addition, IG-based interpretability further shows577
that the model relies on these disease-relevant networks578
when making AD decisions, providing alignment with clin-579
ically recognized neurocognitive mechanisms and enhanc-580
ing the trustworthiness of the proposed framework from a581
clinical interpretation perspective.582
5. Conclusion583
In this work, we presented GeoMAMBA-AD, a John584
ellipsoid-based blind source separation and Mamba-based585
spatiotemporal learning framework for Alzheimer’s dis-586
ease detection from small-sample rs-fMRI. By introduc-587
ing individual-level JE-BSS, selective state-space feature588
learning, and temporal conditioning, the proposed method589
achieves high disease separability (92% OA) and main-590
tains robust discriminative performance under heteroge-591
neous small-data and mixed-scanner conditions. Moreover,592
IG-based analysis identifies functional networks and con-593
trol circuits (e.g., FPN, FCS, and FST) previously linked594
to AD, supporting the neurobiological relevance of the ge-595
ometry sources. Overall, integrating geometric priors with596
Mamba offers a promising direction toward clinically reli-597
able rs-fMRI early AD detection.598
