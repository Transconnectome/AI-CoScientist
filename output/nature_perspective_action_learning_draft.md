## 3. Perspective Draft (~2,800 words)

### Abstract

Learning in the brain is not a passive imprinting of sensory inputs but an active, embodied process driven by behaviour. We argue that volitional action constitutes the brain’s primordial learning signal: by launching predictions into the world, comparing them with ensuing sensory consequences, and iteratively reorganising its own circuitry, the brain transforms acting into delayed, embodied thinking. Drawing on recent systems neuroscience, developmental evidence, and predictive processing theory, we synthesise how exploratory behaviour sculpts neural assemblies, how hippocampal replay consolidates action-episode sequences, and how these dynamics underpin adaptive cognition. We propose that educational and rehabilitative practices should be restructured to maximise opportunities for exploratory action and externalisation, reframing “learning by doing” as an evolutionary imperative and a design principle for both brains and learning environments.

### Introduction: Rethinking the Brain’s Learning Algorithm

The prevailing metaphor of the brain as a passive receiver of information has been steadily overturned. Contemporary neuroscience increasingly reveals the brain as a self-organising, dynamic system whose intrinsic activity and behavioural outputs are not mere end-products but engines of learning themselves [Cha 2025, slides 1–8; Buzsáki 2019, Ch.1]. This reconceptualisation aligns with an “inside-out” perspective: cognition is not constructed solely from the outside in, by accretion of sensory data, but emerges from cycles of internally generated action, prediction, and feedback [Buzsáki 2019, Ch.1].

Yet, most models of learning—both in neuroscience and artificial intelligence—continue to privilege perception, treating action as a downstream consequence. Here, we advance an alternative: that action is the brain’s primordial learning signal. Behaviour is not simply an endpoint but the launchpad for prediction, the context for perception, and the substrate for neural plasticity. In this view, “acting” is delayed thinking—a process through which the brain externalises, tests, and refines its models of the world.

We synthesise evidence from developmental science, systems neuroscience, and computational theory to argue that action-driven inference is the foundational learning algorithm of the brain. This perspective mandates a shift not only in basic research but in the design of educational and rehabilitative environments, privileging exploratory action and the externalisation of thought as engines of adaptive intelligence.

### Action as Delayed Thought: Behaviour, Prediction, and Neural Assembly

#### Action First: Developmental and Mechanistic Evidence

Infant development offers a privileged window into the primacy of action. Before infants can parse the meaning of their sensory world, they move—reaching, grasping, and manipulating objects. These actions are not simply motor babble; they are the means by which the infant reduces sensory ambiguity and constructs internal models [Cha 2025, slides 9–14; Buzsáki 2019, Ch.5]. Evidence from mirror neuron systems, corollary discharge, and the Troxler effect converge on a central insight: without action-contingent updating, sensory meaning collapses [Buzsáki 2019, Ch.3; Evidence Capsule 0.66].

For example, the corollary discharge signals accompanying self-generated movement allow the brain to distinguish reafferent (self-caused) from exafferent (externally caused) sensory changes. Classic phenomena such as the phi illusion and fixation fading underscore this point: when the eyes remain motionless, perceptual stability degrades, but saccades restore coherence by actively sampling and updating predictions [Buzsáki 2019, Ch.3]. Thus, perception is scaffolded by action, not the reverse.

#### Behaviour as Prediction Engine

This action-first principle extends to the neural substrate. When an organism acts, it launches a cascade of predictions—about the expected sensory consequences and the likely outcomes of its behaviours. Errors between predicted and actual outcomes are not noise; they are the fuel for synaptic change. In this sense, behaviour is the brain’s way of externalising hypotheses, using the world as a testbed for inference [Buzsáki 2019, Ch.13].

#### Play and the Sculpting of Cell Assemblies

Exploratory play is the crucible in which cell assemblies—the fundamental units of neural syntax—are forged. During unstructured play, Hebbian co-activation binds together distributed neurons into reusable assemblies, providing the substrate for later abstraction and creativity [Cha 2025, slides 17–20; Buzsáki 2019, Ch.7]. The “meaning” of a neural trajectory is acquired not through rote exposure, but through the behavioural significance attached during active exploration [Buzsáki 2019, Ch.13; Evidence Capsule 0.63]. Birdsong, grooming, and navigation all exemplify how repeated, self-generated action sequences become internalised as flexible cognitive tools.

### Embodied Predictive Coding Across Scales

#### From Sensorimotor Loops to Cognitive Maps

Predictive coding models posit that the brain continually generates predictions about incoming sensory signals and updates them in light of error signals. Yet, these models often overlook the embodied origins of prediction: the repertoire of possible actions shapes the space of plausible predictions [Buzsáki 2019, Ch.13; Evidence Capsule 0.65]. The mirror neuron system, for instance, evolved to map observed actions onto one’s own motor repertoire, suggesting that even high-level social inference is grounded in embodied simulation [Buzsáki 2019, Ch.5; Evidence Capsule 0.65].

At the systems level, cortico-hippocampal circuits decouple from immediate input to simulate potential futures, but these simulations are constructed atop prior action sequences [Buzsáki 2019, Ch.5]. Thus, cognition is scaffolded by the “grammar” of cell assemblies shaped during exploration.

#### Computational and Developmental Perspectives

Computational models that decouple perception from action often struggle with generalisation and transfer. In contrast, embodied models—where agents learn by acting—exhibit greater flexibility and robustness [Needed: Smith & Gasser, 2005; Clark, 2013]. Developmentally, the transition from sensorimotor exploration to abstract reasoning is mirrored in the progressive internalisation of action-generated predictions [Cha 2025, slides 17–20; Buzsáki 2019, Ch.5]. This trajectory suggests that the neural syntax for abstraction is constructed from the raw material of exploratory behaviour.

#### Open Questions

While the evidence for action-driven predictive coding is compelling, mechanistic links between specific action contingencies and the formation of cell assemblies remain to be fully elucidated in humans. Longitudinal studies tracking the evolution of neural assemblies from infancy through adulthood would provide crucial validation [Open question].

### Rest, Replay, and the Design of Experiential Niches

#### Offline Replay as Optimisation

Experience is not consolidated in real time but is reworked during offline states. Hippocampal sharp wave ripples (SWRs) are synchronous replay events during rest and sleep that reactivate recent action sequences, selectively strengthening or weakening synaptic connections [Cha 2025, slides 21–24; Buzsáki 2019, Ch.8; Evidence Capsule 0.69]. Replay is not a rote recapitulation but a generative process: it can reorder, compress, or even invent novel associations, supporting generalisation and transfer [Buzsáki 2019, Ch.8].

Replay events reflect a transition state between randomness and synchrony, providing a substrate for both the consolidation of specific episodes and the discovery of new relational structures [Evidence Capsule 0.67]. This mechanism explains how action-driven learning is optimised offline, linking specific behavioural episodes to long-term cognitive models.

#### Designing for Exploration: Educational and Social Niches

The implications are profound for the design of learning environments. Educators, therapists, and caregivers should be reconceived as facilitators of exploratory niches, not mere transmitters of information [Cha 2025, slides 26–38]. Trust, unstructured play, externalisation of thought (e.g., drawing, storytelling), and collective scaffolding transform individual action into shared intelligence [Buzsáki 2019, Ch.9]. Empirical work suggests that excessive instruction can limit spontaneous exploration and discovery [Needed: Bonawitz et al., 2011], while minimal guidance may be insufficient for some learners [Needed: Kirschner et al., 2006]. The challenge is to engineer environments that optimise the balance between freedom and structure, maximising opportunities for action-driven inference.

#### Open Questions

How best to measure and scaffold exploratory action in diverse learners remains an open area for translational research. Interventions that selectively manipulate replay dynamics, such as targeted sleep or rest protocols, offer promising avenues for enhancing consolidation in both typical and clinical populations [Open question].

### Implications for Education and Rehabilitation

#### Educational Design

If action is the brain’s primary learning signal, curricula should prioritise opportunities for volitional, exploratory behaviour. This extends from early childhood—where unstructured play is critical—to higher education and adult learning, where externalisation and collective problem-solving drive innovation [Cha 2025, slides 26–38; Buzsáki 2019, Ch.9]. Technologies that enable embodied interaction (e.g., robotics, virtual reality) may offer novel platforms for amplifying action-driven learning.

#### Rehabilitation and Neuroplasticity

In rehabilitation, action-driven paradigms may enhance recovery by leveraging the brain’s intrinsic capacity to reorganise through behaviour. For example, task-specific training and constraint-induced movement therapy exploit Hebbian principles to drive plasticity in injured circuits. Interventions that integrate rest and replay—such as structured sleep or offline consolidation—could further potentiate recovery by optimising the binding of new action repertoires [Buzsáki 2019, Ch.8].

#### Social and Collective Intelligence

Finally, the externalisation of thought—through language, shared artefacts, and collaborative play—transforms individual learning into collective intelligence. This principle underlies the rapid evolution of culture and technology, suggesting that the design of social environments is as crucial as neural mechanisms for fostering adaptive cognition [Buzsáki 2019, Ch.9].

### Conclusion: Evolutionary Lessons from Action-Driven Learning

The brain’s evolutionary strategy is clear: act first, learn by doing, and consolidate through replay. By externalising predictions into behaviour, the brain harnesses the world as a training ground, sculpting flexible, adaptive circuits through cycles of action, feedback, and rest. This perspective demands a shift in both scientific inquiry and educational design, reframing “learning by doing” not as a pedagogical slogan but as an evolutionary imperative built into the architecture of the brain.

Future research should prioritise the mechanistic mapping of action–prediction–consolidation loops across scales, from cell assemblies to social systems. Translational efforts must focus on creating environments—biological, technological, and social—that maximise opportunities for action-driven inference. In this light, acting is not simply a consequence of thinking; it is thinking, delayed, embodied, and made adaptive through the relentless interplay of prediction and feedback.

## 4. Future Research Agenda

1. **Longitudinal Mapping of Action–Assembly Dynamics in Infancy**  
   - *Hypothesis*: Early exploratory actions drive the formation and consolidation of specific cell assemblies, which scaffold later abstract cognition.  
   - *Method*: Use longitudinal EEG/MEG and behavioural tracking in infants, correlating motor exploration with evolving neural assembly patterns and later cognitive outcomes.  
   - *Expected Contribution*: Direct evidence for the causal role of action in structuring neural syntax for abstraction.

2. **Manipulating Replay to Enhance Learning**  
   - *Hypothesis*: Targeted enhancement of hippocampal replay (e.g., via closed-loop stimulation during sleep/rest) will selectively consolidate action-derived learning episodes.  
   - *Method*: Use rodent or human participants in navigation or skill-learning tasks; apply real-time detection and modulation of SWRs during post-learning rest.  
   - *Expected Contribution*: Mechanistic link between replay events and consolidation of action-driven learning; potential clinical applications in rehabilitation.

3. **Embodied Predictive Coding in Artificial Agents**  
   - *Hypothesis*: Agents that learn by acting (action-driven predictive coding) will outperform passive learners in generalisation and transfer.  
   - *Method*: Implement embodied reinforcement learning agents with and without action-contingent prediction modules; compare performance on transfer and abstraction tasks.  
   - *Expected Contribution*: Computational validation of embodied predictive coding; insights for AI and robotics.

4. **Educational Intervention: Action-Driven Curriculum Design**  
   - *Hypothesis*: Curricula structured to maximise exploratory action and externalisation will outperform traditional instruction in fostering deep learning and creativity.  
   - *Method*: Randomised controlled trials in classroom settings comparing action-driven vs. didactic learning environments; outcome measures include creativity, transfer, and retention
