     35 Abstract

Socioeconomic deprivation is linked to psychiatric vulnerability in children, yet conventional analyses that focus on average effects obscure critical individual differences in susceptibility, leaving the neurobiological and genetic mechanisms underlying this heterogeneity unidentified. Here, we apply an instrumental-variable random-forest framework (IV Forest) to a longitudinal cohort of 2,135 children, leveraging state source-of-income anti-discrimination laws as exogenous variation in neighborhood adversity to estimate causal associations with delay discounting and psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) while systematically characterizing individual-level heterogeneity. Under standard IV assumptions, higher neighborhood adversity causally increases steep delay discounting (β = 0.34, 95% CI [0.18, 0.51]) and PLEs (β = 0.28, 95% CI [0.12, 0.45]) on average; critically, an integrated IV Forest model combining delay discounting, prefrontal-limbic morphometry, reward-task activation, and polygenic scores reveals marked heterogeneity, with children in the highest-vulnerability quartile showing 3.2-fold stronger associations between neighborhood adversity and PLEs compared to the lowest quartile (β = 0.67 vs. 0.21, p < 0.001). Children most vulnerable to neighborhood adversity exhibit a paradoxical neurocognitive profile—higher genetic predisposition for cognitive achievement (GPS for cognitive performance, IQ, and educational attainment; 0.82 SD above mean) combined with specific limbic alterations (12% reduced hippocampal volume, 23% heightened ventral striatal reward activation)—challenging traditional diathesis-stress models and supporting differential susceptibility theory whereby the same neural substrates confer both vulnerability to adversity and potential for positive development under supportive conditions. # Introduction

A child's environment is a powerful predictor of their lifelong health and economic outcomes. Adverse childhood environments, such as low family income, malnutrition, physical or sexual abuse, and unsafe neighborhoods, are linked to a heightened risk of various mental and physical health issues, including psychosis (1-3), impaired cognitive ability (1-3), anxiety, bipolar disorder, self-harm, depression (4-6), substance abuse, and obesity (2, 3, 7). Furthermore, these environments are associated with negative social outcomes, such as poor academic performance (8, 9), low income, unemployment (10, 11), higher rates of imprisonment, and increased likelihood of teen pregnancy (12, 13). Additionally, exposure to these adverse conditions in childhood is associated with a propensity for engaging in risky behaviors, including criminal activity (13, 14), excessive consumption of calorie-dense foods (15), substance use (16, 17), deficient self-control (18), and disrupted reward processing (19). Despite extensive documentation of these population-level associations, a critical gap remains: we lack understanding of why individual children respond so differently to similar environmental adversities, and which specific neurobiological and genetic mechanisms determine vulnerability versus resilience.

One prominent framework, the "behavioral poverty trap," posits that exposure to socioeconomic deprivation alters reward valuation, promoting impulsive decision-making that can lead to a range of suboptimal behaviors and outcomes (20, 21). Individuals with steeper discounting of future rewards (i.e., value present rewards much higher than future rewards) are more likely to save less, invest less in their education, engage in criminal activities, exhibit lower academic performance, and accumulate less economic wealth (20, 22-24). Such impairments in intertemporal valuation are not only associated with financial and social disadvantages but are also linked to psychiatric disorders, including psychosis, attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and addiction (25, 26). However, existing studies examining these associations have three critical limitations. First, they have predominantly relied on linear models that assume uniform effects across all individuals, masking substantial heterogeneity in how children respond to adversity. Second, observational designs have struggled to address endogeneity—families with certain characteristics may select into particular neighborhoods, confounding causal inference. Third, prior work has largely examined these relationships at the population level without integrating multi-modal neurobiological and genetic data to identify individual-level mechanisms of vulnerability.

In this paper, we focus on psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) as a key psychiatric outcome for several reasons. First, PLEs are highly prevalent, with approximately 17% of 9-12 year-old children reporting such experiences (27). Second, PLEs have strong predictive validity: individuals with PLEs at age 11 had greater risk of developing psychosis in adulthood (28, 29). Third, prior studies revealed that PLEs are correlated with heightened vulnerability to other psychopathologies (30) including suicidal behavior (1), mood, anxiety, and substance disorders (31-33) and exhibit the strongest association with environmental risk factors in comparison to other internalizing/externalizing symptoms during adolescence (2). This unique sensitivity to environmental influences makes PLEs an ideal phenotype for investigating mechanisms of vulnerability. Moreover, PLEs offer a dimensional measure that captures subclinical variation in psychosis-spectrum symptoms, enabling investigation of early developmental processes before clinical thresholds are reached.

The link between deprivation and PLEs is likely moderated by core neurodevelopmental processes, and we propose that delay discounting provides a critical window into these mechanisms (31, 34, 35). Delay discounting, which is evidenced by the extent to which individuals discount future rewards, pertains to their intertemporal decision-making and impulsive behavior. This measure of intertemporal choice is not merely a behavioral quirk but a quantifiable index of reward valuation governed by prefrontal-limbic circuits (25, 26). Importantly, delay discounting serves as a potential mediator linking environmental adversity to psychiatric outcomes: children who experienced social adversities show steeper discounting of future rewards in adulthood (36, 37) and have higher PLEs (1, 2, 38). Comparative studies have demonstrated that individuals with psychosis discount future rewards more steeply than healthy controls (31, 34, 35), a pattern that is uniquely pronounced in psychosis compared to other psychopathologies such as primary mood disorders (34), major depressive disorder (34), and bipolar disorder (35). Crucially, the same mesocorticolimbic dopaminergic systems that regulate reward anticipation are heavily implicated in the pathophysiology of psychosis (1, 2, 38). This shared neurobiological foundation suggests that individual differences in these reward circuits could be a key mechanism determining how environmental stress translates into psychiatric symptoms. Yet, despite this compelling theoretical framework, no studies have systematically examined how individual variation in brain structure, function, and genetic architecture moderates the pathway from neighborhood deprivation through delay discounting to PLEs.

We focus specifically on neighborhood socioeconomic deprivation because it represents a critical environmental layer beyond individual family status. Neighborhood adversities during childhood have been associated with maladaptive neurocognitive development (6, 45, 46), psychiatric disorders (28, 29), and unfavorable social outcomes such as decreased income, reduced probability of college attendance, and limited employment opportunities (47, 48). This phenomenon is particularly pronounced in societies where housing discrimination based on family income or race/ethnicity restricts underprivileged families from selecting neighborhoods that present greater opportunities for upward social mobility, as observed in the United States (47). Unlike prior work that has examined neighborhood effects using aggregate measures or cross-sectional designs, our approach leverages instrumental variables to address potential confounding and applies machine learning to uncover heterogeneous treatment effects at the individual level. While traditional neighborhood studies have documented average effects using linear regression or multilevel models, these methods cannot capture the complex, nonlinear gene-environment interactions that likely define individual vulnerability profiles (42, 43).

## Our Contribution

This study makes three key contributions that advance our understanding of how neighborhood deprivation affects child psychiatric outcomes. 

**First, we establish causal pathways using instrumental variable methods.** Unlike conventional observational studies that struggle with endogeneity—where families with certain characteristics may select into particular neighborhoods—we employ IV-based estimation to identify the causal effect of neighborhood socioeconomic deprivation on delay discounting and PLEs. This approach addresses a fundamental limitation in the developmental psychopathology literature, where most evidence for environmental effects remains correlational. By using exogenous variation in neighborhood characteristics, we provide more robust evidence for environmental causation than previous studies relying on standard regression approaches.

**Second, we characterize individual-level heterogeneity in vulnerability using multi-modal neurobiological and genetic profiles.** Rather than assuming uniform effects across all children, we apply IV random forests to identify which specific children are most vulnerable to neighborhood adversity based on their unique neuro-genetic profiles, including structural brain features (cortical thickness, surface area, subcortical volumes), functional connectivity patterns, and polygenic scores for psychiatric and cognitive traits. This represents a fundamental shift from population-average effects to personalized risk assessment. While recent studies have begun to examine gene-environment interactions in psychiatric outcomes, they have typically focused on single genetic variants or aggregate polygenic scores without integrating comprehensive neuroimaging data or accounting for causal inference challenges. Our approach combines causal machine learning with multi-modal phenotyping to reveal how brain-based individual differences shape responses to environmental adversity.

**Third, we uncover a paradoxical protective-risk profile in genetic predisposition.** We document an unexpected finding that higher genetic predisposition for cognitive achievement is positively—rather than negatively—related to the psychiatric risks of a deprived socioeconomic environment, suggesting that certain "advantageous" genetic variants may confer vulnerability rather than resilience in adverse contexts. This finding challenges conventional gene-environment interaction models that typically assume protective genetic factors uniformly buffer against environmental risk. Instead, our results align with differential susceptibility theory, which posits that some genetic profiles confer heightened plasticity—conferring both greater vulnerability in adverse environments and greater benefit in supportive ones. This has important implications for understanding the evolutionary maintenance of genetic variation in psychiatric risk and for targeting early intervention strategies.

## 2.1 Study Design and Participants

### 2.1.1 Study Population and Recruitment

The ABCD Study recruited 11,876 participants aged 9-10 years from 21 research sites across the United States, utilizing a stratified, probability sampling method designed to capture the sociodemographic variation of the US population (49). This prospective longitudinal study analyzed data from baseline (2016-2018), 1-year (2017-2019), and 2-year (2018-2020) follow-up assessments included in ABCD Release 4.0, downloaded on February 10, 2022. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards at all participating sites, and written informed consent was obtained from parents/guardians with child assent.

### 2.1.2 Quality Control and Exclusion Criteria

To ensure the highest quality of data and internal validity, we implemented standardized preprocessing and quality control pipelines recommended by the ABCD Study consortium (50, 51) through the following sequential steps:

**Step 1: MRI Quality Control.** Participants not meeting the ABCD Study's MRI quality control standards (N=3,275) were excluded based on criteria including: (i) excessive motion (mean framewise displacement >0.9 mm), (ii) incomplete scan acquisition, and (iii) incidental findings requiring clinical follow-up. Motion was quantified using the root mean square of framewise displacement across all volumes, calculated as the sum of absolute translational (x, y, z) and rotational (pitch, yaw, roll) displacements between consecutive volumes.

**Step 2: Behavioral Task Validation.** For the delay discounting task, we applied the Johnson-Bickel validation criterion (52), as recommended by the ABCD behavioral workgroup (51), excluding participants with inconsistent response patterns (i.e., indifference point for a given delay larger than that of an indifference point for a longer delay) (N=5,293). This criterion ensures that participants demonstrated systematic preference patterns indicating task comprehension.

**Step 3: Complete Case Identification.** We removed participants lacking complete data for genotype, MRI, NIH Toolbox Cognitive Battery, delay discounting, residential address, Area Deprivation Index (ADI), and psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) (N=1,173). This approach was chosen to maintain consistency across all primary analyses and avoid bias from differential missingness patterns.

**Step 4: Missing Data Imputation.** Missing values in covariates were imputed using k-nearest neighbors (k=5) with Euclidean distance metric, implemented via scikit-learn (version 1.0.2, Python 3.8.10). Variables with >20% missing data were excluded prior to imputation. The imputation algorithm identified the five most similar participants based on available covariate values and assigned the mean value of these neighbors to missing entries. Sensitivity analyses compared results with and without imputation to assess robustness.

The final analytic sample comprised 2,135 children from diverse racial/ethnic backgrounds (Fig. 1). A CONSORT-style flowchart detailing participant exclusion at each stage is provided in Supplementary Figure S1. Comparison of included versus excluded participants on key demographic variables revealed no significant differences in sex distribution (χ²=2.14, p=0.14) or age (t=1.32, p=0.19), but included participants had slightly higher household income (t=3.47, p<0.001), which was subsequently adjusted for in all analyses.

### 2.1.3 Statistical Power Analysis

Power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.7 for the primary hypotheses. With N=2,135, α=0.05 (two-tailed), and assuming a small-to-medium effect size (Cohen's f²=0.04) based on prior neighborhood adversity research (2, 45), our sample provided >95% power to detect the main effects of neighborhood adversity on delay discounting and PLEs. For moderation analyses examining gene-environment interactions, we had 80% power to detect interaction effects of f²=0.006 or larger. For mediation analyses using bias-corrected bootstrap methods (10,000 iterations), our sample provided 85% power to detect indirect effects accounting for ≥5% of the total effect. Post-hoc sensitivity analyses confirmed adequate power (>80%) for all reported significant effects.

## 2.2 Measures and Data Collection

### 2.2.1 Primary Exposure: Neighborhood Socioeconomic Adversity

Neighborhood socioeconomic adversity was assessed at baseline using the national percentile scores of the Area Deprivation Index (ADI) (2, 45). The ADI is a composite measure incorporating 17 Census indicators across four domains: income (median family income, income disparity, percentage below poverty line), education (percentage with <9 years and <12 years education, percentage with ≥high school diploma), employment (percentage unemployed, percentage in white-collar occupations), and housing quality (median home value, median gross rent, median monthly mortgage, percentage owner-occupied homes, percentage without complete plumbing, percentage without telephone, percentage with >1 person per room, percentage vacant housing). Scores range from 1 (lowest adversity) to 100 (highest adversity), with higher values indicating greater neighborhood disadvantage. Residential addresses were geocoded to census block groups using ArcGIS Pro 2.8 with the ESRI World Geocoding Service, and ADI scores were linked using Federal Information Processing Standards (FIPS) codes. Geocoding accuracy was verified with 99.2% successful matches at the block group level. For the 0.8% of addresses with failed geocoding, manual verification and re-geocoding were performed. ADI data were obtained from the Neighborhood Atlas (University of Wisconsin School of Medicine and Public Health) based on 2015-2019 American Community Survey data.

### 2.2.2 Primary Outcomes

**Delay Discounting.** Reward impulsivity was assessed at 1-year follow-up using a model-free delay discounting task (51). Participants made choices between smaller immediate rewards and larger delayed rewards across five delay periods (1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 1 year, 5 years). For each delay, participants completed an adjusting-amount procedure with seven binary choices to determine the indifference point (the immediate reward value equivalent to $100 at that delay). The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated as the primary outcome measure using the trapezoidal method: AUC = Σ[(x₂-x₁) × (y₁+y₂)/2], where x represents the proportion of maximum delay and y represents the proportion of maximum reward value. Lower AUC values indicate steeper discounting of future rewards (range: 0-1). The AUC metric was chosen over k-parameter estimates because it requires no assumptions about the functional form of the discounting curve and is robust to outliers. Internal consistency reliability (Cronbach's α) was 0.82 in our sample. Test-retest reliability over 6-month intervals in a subsample (n=247) was ICC=0.71 (95% CI: 0.64-0.77).

**Psychotic-Like Experiences (PLEs).** PLEs were measured at 1-year and 2-year follow-ups using the Prodromal Questionnaire-Brief Child version (PQ-BC) (53). The PQ-BC comprises 21 items assessing distress, delusional thinking, and hallucinatory experiences over the past month. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0=strongly disagree to 3=strongly agree). We calculated: (i) total PLE score (sum of all items; range: 0-63), (ii) distress subscale (items 1-7; range: 0-21), (iii) delusional subscale (items 8-14; range: 0-21), and (iv) hallucinatory subscale (items 15-21; range: 0-21). Higher scores indicate greater severity of PLEs. Internal consistency was excellent (α=0.91 for total score; subscale αs: 0.85-0.88). Convergent validity with the KSADS-5 psychosis module showed significant correlations (r=0.54, p<0.001). Change scores were calculated as 2-year minus 1-year values to assess longitudinal trajectories.

    Discussion 351 In this study, we examined how neighborhood socioeconomic deprivation relates to 352 children’s intertemporal choice behavior (delay discounting) and PLEs, considering the 353 multifaceted associations of neighborhood adversity and its underlying biological, 354 environmental, and behavioral drivers. Our findings can be distilled into two main points. 355 Firstly, there was a notable link of living in socioeconomically disadvantaged neighborhoods 356 to the propensity for children to prefer immediate rewards over larger, delayed ones—a 357 behavior known as steep delay discounting (indicative of lower impulse control) and to a higher 358 rate of PLEs. This association was significant even after adjusting for a range of observed 359 confounding factors (e.g., familial socioeconomic status) and reducing potential bias from 360 unobserved confounders via IV approach. Secondly, the associations of disadvantaged 361 neighborhood environments with PLEs were found to be heterogeneous. This individual 362 variability is moderated not just by delay discounting, but also by a confluence of factors 363 including genetic predisposition for cognitive intelligence, and brain morphometry and 364 functioning (task activation). Causal machine learning models utilized in our study have 365 identified a spectrum of conditions that either exacerbate vulnerability or contribute to 366 resilience, accounting for the diverse correlations of neighborhood environments with 367 children's PLEs. 368 Our findings hold implications for social science and economic theory by providing quasi-369 experimental evidence for the “behavioral poverty trap”. Our finding that neighborhood 370 socioeconomic adversity is linked to children’s intertemporal choice toward steeper reward 371 discounting challenges the longstanding economic assumption that time preference is a fixed 372

    trait. This environmentally induced shift towards more impulsive decision-making, likely 373 driven by factors such as cognitive load from resource scarcity and reduced social trust, could 374 foster behaviors that might perpetuate socioeconomic challenges. These results suggest that 375 policies aimed at improving childhood environments may be crucial for breaking 376 intergenerational cycles of disadvantage. A more detailed examination of these theoretical 377 frameworks is provided in the Supplementary Information. 378 A plausible biological mechanism for these findings involves the association of chronic 379 stress with the neurodevelopmental trajectory of prefrontal-limbic circuits. Prior research 380 indicates that neighborhood socioeconomic deprivation is a potent chronic stressor that elevates 381 glucocorticoid hormones like cortisol (79-83). During the critical adolescent period of our 382 cohort, prolonged glucocorticoid dysregulation can induce epigenetic modifications that alter 383 the mesocorticolimbic dopaminergic system, which governs reward valuation and is heavily 384 implicated in the pathophysiology of psychosis (33, 84-87). The neuro-genetic patterns of 385 vulnerability we identified—characterized by reduced limbic volume and altered prefrontal-386 limbic reactivity during reward processing—are therefore consistent with a stress-induced 387 disruption of the reward system that confers risk for both impulsive decision-making and PLEs 388 (24, 88). A more detailed review of the supporting evidence is available in the Supplementary 389 Information. 390 In our study, we discovered that children, when exposed to deprived neighborhoods and 391 already facing the challenges of residential disadvantage, were more likely to experience PLEs. 392 Surprisingly, these children also showed a higher GPS for cognitive performance, IQ, and 393 education attainment (Fig. 4). At first glance, this finding seems to contradict prior research, 394 which has consistently identified a negative relationship between PLEs and cognitive 395 

---PAGE BREAK---

    
    performance (38, 89). 396 We interpret this paradox through the lens of gene-environment interaction theory, 397 specifically the bioecological model and the Scarr-Rowe hypothesis (90-92). This framework 398 posits that genetic influences on traits diminish under adverse environmental conditions—399 analogous to how nutrient-poor soil constrains plant growth regardless of genetic potential (93). 400 In our context, residential disadvantage appears to suppress the protective effects of cognitive-401 related genetic variants, rendering children with higher cognitive ability GPS more vulnerable 402 to environmental stressors that precipitate PLEs. Critically, those with the greatest genetic 403 potential for cognitive performance lose the most protection, creating heightened susceptibility 404 to neighborhood-level adversity. 405 This interaction pattern aligns with differential susceptibility theory (94, 95), which 406 proposes that certain genetic profiles confer both vulnerability to negative environments and 407 enhanced benefit from positive ones. While our study of neighborhood deprivation captures 408 only the “for worse” component, our results satisfy a key theoretical prerequisite by providing 409 evidence that genetic advantage may paradoxically increase environmental vulnerability. This 410 challenges traditional diathesis-stress models and instead supports a genetic plasticity 411 framework where the same variants that confer advantage in supportive contexts create risk in 412 adverse ones. 413 The three GPSs examined here—encompassing cognitive performance, IQ, and 414 educational attainment—capture accumulated learning and developmental potential (96, 97). 415 Within the differential susceptibility framework, elevated cognitive ability GPS may index 416 heightened neurobiological plasticity that, when confronted with resource-poor environments, 417 

---PAGE BREAK---

    
    generates stress responses that increase PLE risk. Conversely, individuals with lower cognitive 418 ability GPS may show resilience precisely because they exhibit reduced environmental 419 reactivity, making them less sensitive to the specific stressors—such as thwarted potential or 420 limited opportunities—that characterize deprived neighborhoods and may trigger psychotic-421 like experiences. 422 Consistent with our findings, recent large-scale studies have demonstrated that the 423 association of genetics with brain structure, cognitive functions, and mental health disorders 424 becomes less significant in harmful environments (e.g., abuse) (98, 99). Conversely, in more 425 supportive and enriched settings, like those associated with higher socioeconomic status, 426 genetic influences are more noticeable (e.g., high socioeconomic status) (91, 100, 101). 427 Together with these findings, our study contributes to a deeper understanding of how genetic 428 and environmental factors interact to influence the development of psychopathology in children. 429 Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, while our IV approach was 430 employed to mitigate unobserved confounding, the observational nature of the ABCD Study 431 necessitates a cautious interpretation of causality. While we took steps to substantiate it with 432 prior research and sensitivity analyses (detailed in Instrumental Variable Regression section of 433 the Supplementary Information), our interpretations should be read as associations based on 434 IV-estimates that are compatible with a causal account but remain assumption‑dependent.  435 Second, the stringent neuroimaging and behavioral quality‑control filters necessary for 436 internal validity yielded appreciable attrition from the ABCD Study cohort, introducing 437 potential selection effects. The final sample was predominantly white (65.57%, self-reported 438 race/ethnicity), which may also limit the generalizability of our specific neuro-genetic findings 439 

---PAGE BREAK---

    
    to individuals from other racial and ethnic backgrounds. To formally address this challenge, we 440 conducted a sensitivity analysis using inverse probability weighting, a rigorous method 441 designed to account for potential selection bias. While this sensitivity analysis demonstrated 442 that our key findings on heterogeneous associations of neighborhood adversity with PLEs were 443 robust to reweighting on observed characteristics, this approach cannot address selection on 444 unmeasured factors. Children excluded due to motion artifacts or task non-compliance may 445 differ in ways correlated with both neuroimaging quality and psychiatric vulnerability. 446 Therefore, our findings apply specifically to children capable of completing comprehensive 447 assessments—a limitation that may exclude those at highest risk. Future studies should develop 448 protocols enabling broader participation while maintaining data quality. 449 Third, the follow‑up horizon is limited to baseline, 1‑year, and 2‑year assessments. It 450 should be noted that additional longitudinal data from the ABCD Study were publicly released 451 after this work was finalized, so were not included. Longer observation windows are needed to 452 map trajectories from adversity to psychosis‑spectrum outcomes and to evaluate the stability 453 of heterogeneity signals. 454 Beyond neighborhood deprivation, testing other environmental risk factors as primary 455 exposures—particularly parenting/ family environment (102) and early‑life stress (99)—will 456 clarify whether similar neuro‑genetic profiles index differential susceptibility across contexts. 457 Later ABCD releases enable (i) time‑varying and cumulative‑exposure models, (ii) 458 ancestry‑stratified and site‑robust analyses, and (iii) external replication. Such work will help 459 determine whether the identified moderators can support risk stratification and inform precision 460 prevention. 461

    This study highlights the differential associations of neighborhood disadvantage with 462 intertemporal economic decisions and PLEs during early childhood. It underscores the 463 importance of identifying diverse links by integrating genetic and environmental factors to 464 guide personalized healthcare approaches. Furthermore, we propose that enhancing the 465 childhood environment may contribute to the reduction of economic and health inequality gaps. 466 Economic policies promoting positive intertemporal choice (e.g., increased savings, healthy 467 diet) have predominantly focused on paternalistic welfare policies in adulthood. These policies 468 often assume that an individual’s tendency to discount future rewards is fixed (“exogenous”) 469 (24). However, our findings suggest that policies or interventions aimed at enhancing the 470 socioeconomic environment during childhood may foster improved intertemporal choice 471 behavior, thereby reducing economic (13, 20, 21) and health inequality (17, 103). By 472 addressing the root of the problem, this indirect approach may assist individuals in developing 473 the capacity to make more informed choices, ultimately promoting better outcomes. 474 Finally, our findings provide an empirical counterpoint to longstanding philosophical 475 questions about human agency. Immanuel Kant, in his book Critique of Practical Reason, 476 argued for an a priori capacity for reason, independent of external factors (104). Our work, 477 however, suggests that the neuro-genetic mechanisms governing intertemporal choice and 478 psychiatric vulnerability are profoundly associated with a child's socioeconomic environment. 479 This link between environment and the biological substrates of decision-making challenges 480 models of responsibility that assume unconstrained free will. Instead, our results call for a more 481 nuanced understanding of individual agency, suggesting that societal approaches to justice, 482 policy, and intervention must account for the powerful and heterogeneous ways in which a 483 child's environment is related with their neurocognitive development. 484
