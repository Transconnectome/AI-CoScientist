Socioeconomic deprivation increases psychiatric vulnerability in children, yet individual susceptibility varies markedly. We examined how neighborhood disadvantage affects 2,135 children (ages 9-10, followed 2 years) using instrumental-variable random forests—a causal inference method leveraging state housing anti-discrimination laws as natural experiments. Higher neighborhood adversity increased both impulsive decision-making and psychotic-like experiences (early psychosis symptoms), with substantial variation across individuals. These patterns support behavioral poverty traps: poverty-induced stress promotes decisions perpetuating economic hardship.

Integrating brain imaging, reward-task responses, and genetic data, we identified which children were most vulnerable. Surprisingly, those at highest risk showed a paradoxical profile: elevated genetic predisposition for cognitive achievement combined with specific limbic alterations (reduced volumes, heightened reward activation). This challenges traditional diathesis-stress models, suggesting some children with high cognitive potential are differentially susceptible to environmental adversity.

By revealing who is vulnerable to neighborhood disadvantage and why, these findings enable precision approaches to prevent childhood psychopathology and interrupt cycles of socioeconomic disadvantage.
